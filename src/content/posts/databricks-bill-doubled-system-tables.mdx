The question arrived the way it always does: a screenshot of a cost dashboard, a line going up and
to the right, and "why did Databricks get so expensive?"

The year-over-year number was real. Modeled discounted DBU spend on the platform had more than
doubled (+127%) over the same 267 days of the previous year. The first answer people reach for is
"Databricks raised prices." That answer was mostly wrong, and proving it took a method more than a
query.

This is the playbook I used. Nothing in it needs anything beyond `system.billing`,
`system.compute`, `system.lakeflow` and `system.query`, plus discipline about what the data can
and cannot tell you.

## 1. Fix the comparison before you compute anything

Most bad cost conclusions are wrong before the first `GROUP BY`.

- **Compare matched calendar windows.** Our billing history started in February of the prior
  year and the current month was partial, so the honest comparison was February 1 to October 25
  in both years. Not "this year vs last year", not annualized.
- **Write down the scope.** All workspaces and environments, or production only? Discounted DBU
  cost, list cost, or invoice? We used modeled discounted DBU cost and explicitly excluded AWS
  infrastructure and invoice adjustments, so nobody would compare our number with the finance one.
- **Decide how to treat odd rows.** Rows with a null cost were all `FREE_USAGE` SKUs. They stay in
  DBU totals and are never imputed.

If a stakeholder later disagrees with your number, the disagreement should be about scope you wrote
down, not about arithmetic.

## 2. Reconcile before you explain

The cost dashboard was built on an attributed table: billing records enriched with a solution
(product), a job, a user. Attribution is where cost data lies to you. A record joined twice is a
cost increase that never happened.

So before any explanation, I checked three things:

1. **Attributed totals equal raw totals.** Sum of attributed DBUs per day against
   `system.billing.usage` for the same day.
2. **No missing records.** Every raw billing `record_id` appears in the attributed table.
3. **No double allocation.** Records split across solutions sum back to one.

For the products I investigated, reconciliation found zero missing records and attributed DBUs
matched raw DBUs exactly. That is a boring result, and it is the most important one: it means every
later finding is about consumption, not about bookkeeping.

## 3. Split volume from rate

Spend is DBUs times price. Before arguing about either, separate them.

At the prior year's blended cost per DBU, the extra DBUs explain one part of the increase. The
residual is the "rate and mix" effect.

| | Prior | Current | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| DBUs | index 100 | 162 | +62% |
| Spend | index 100 | 227 | +127% |

Volume growth explained roughly half of the increase. The other half was the blended rate rising,
and that is the part people read as "price went up."

It was not. Breaking the rate down by SKU showed the largest driver, serverless SQL, had the **same
list price and the same discount in both periods**. Its DBUs simply grew several times over, and
because serverless SQL costs more per DBU than classic jobs compute, the workload mix shifted the
blended rate upward. Serverless SQL alone drove 78% of the net growth.

Two warnings about this bridge. It is order-dependent (volume first, then rate), and it is
arithmetic, not causality. I label it that way in every report, because "rate/mix residual" will
otherwise be quoted in a meeting as "Databricks raised prices by X%."

```sql
-- Price every usage record at the list price in effect when it was used.
with priced as (
  select
    u.usage_date,
    u.workspace_id,
    u.billing_origin_product            as workload,
    u.sku_name,
    u.usage_metadata.job_id             as job_id,
    u.usage_metadata.job_run_id         as job_run_id,
    u.usage_metadata.cluster_id         as cluster_id,
    u.usage_quantity                    as dbus,
    u.usage_quantity * lp.pricing.default as list_cost
  from system.billing.usage u
  left join system.billing.list_prices lp
    on  u.sku_name   = lp.sku_name
    and u.cloud      = lp.cloud
    and u.usage_unit = lp.usage_unit
    and u.usage_start_time >= lp.price_start_time
    and (lp.price_end_time is null or u.usage_start_time < lp.price_end_time)
),
periods as (
  select *,
    case
      when usage_date between '2025-02-01' and '2025-10-25' then 'prior'
      when usage_date between '2026-02-01' and '2026-10-25' then 'current'
    end as period
  from priced
)
select sku_name, period,
       sum(dbus)                    as dbus,
       sum(list_cost)               as list_cost,
       sum(list_cost) / sum(dbus)   as list_price_per_dbu
from periods
where period is not null
group by sku_name, period;
```

If `list_price_per_dbu` is flat for a SKU across periods, that SKU's growth is consumption, full stop.

## 4. Walk down the hierarchy until the cost concentrates

Platform, then workload type, then SKU, then solution, then job. At each level, look for where the
increase concentrates, not where the spend is largest.

The pattern was striking. Platform growth was broad, but **within a product it was almost always
one or two jobs**:

- In one product, a single production aggregation job explained **90.5%** of the increase.
- In another, three jobs explained **99%** of it, led by a table-maintenance (optimize and vacuum)
  job.

That is the moment an investigation becomes actionable. "SQL is expensive" is a strategy
discussion. "This job" is a ticket with an owner.

## 5. Normalize per run. This is where causes show up

A job's cost can rise because it runs more often or because each run does more work. Billing
records carry `job_run_id`, so you can tell the two apart:

```sql
select job_id, period,
       count(distinct job_run_id)                 as billed_runs,
       sum(dbus)                                  as dbus,
       sum(dbus) / count(distinct job_run_id)     as dbus_per_run
from periods
where job_id is not null and period is not null
group by job_id, period;
```

For the aggregation job, billed runs went slightly **down** year over year while DBUs per run went
up **3.08×**. Frequency was ruled out in one query. Compute metadata (join `cluster_id` to
`system.compute.clusters`) showed the job had moved from a 4xlarge to an 8xlarge worker type in the same instance family. That
is a plausible cause of a sustained step-up, and a cheap thing to test.

The table-maintenance job told the same story more dramatically: roughly **16× more DBUs per run**
with a similar run count. Maintenance jobs are easy to forget once they are scheduled, and they
quietly grow with every table they touch.

## 6. Say what billing cannot tell you

Billing tells you *how much compute*, not *why*. It cannot distinguish more input data from slower
processing, extra workers from longer runs, or retries from real work. For that you need run
history (`system.lakeflow.job_run_timeline`, task timelines) and ideally input sizes.

On this platform my first attempt at run history failed with a permissions error on
`system.lakeflow`. I recorded the failure, kept the query, and wrote "frequency, runtime, retries and
compute sizing remain unverified" in the findings instead of guessing. The later run-level pass
narrowed it down: from one month to the next, the job's average scheduled run took about twice as
long *and* burned more DBUs per elapsed hour. Telling worker scaling apart from data growth still
needs task-level metrics, and the report says so.

A finding labeled "plausible, not proven" that later gets confirmed builds more trust than a
confident guess that gets corrected.

## 7. Make it repeatable, and make it safe

The investigation lived in a small repo, not a notebook session:

- **Numbered, read-only SQL files**, executed in order. The runner rejects multi-statement input
  and anything that is not `SELECT`, `DESCRIBE` or `SHOW`, so an analysis can never mutate
  production.
- **Every result saved with metadata**: execution time, row count, schema, and the error if it
  failed.
- **A summarizer that validates before it reports**: calendar coverage, cross-export totals,
  unique job identities. If the checks fail, there is no report.

Extending the analysis through a later date is a date change and a re-run, not a new
investigation.

## What I would tell anyone starting this

- **Reconcile first.** You cannot explain a number you have not verified.
- **Separate volume from rate before anyone says "price increase."**
- **Per-run normalization is the most useful number in Databricks cost work.** Costs that look like
  growth are usually heavier runs, not more of them.
- **Write the limits into the report.** It is what makes the rest believable.

The longer-term fix was not the investigation itself but making attribution trustworthy by default.
